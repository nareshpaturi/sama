Sama Vritti

Box breathing

▶ Hear it SUH-muh VRIT-tee सम वृत्ति

GentleSeated or lyingShort holds

4 · 4 · 4 · 4 5 min · 19 rounds · 5:04 · guided 3.8 breaths/min

Take care: Keep the holds easy. If one feels tight, shorten it or set it to Off.

Four equal steps: breathe in, pause, breathe out, pause. An even count gives a busy mind something simple to follow.

How to

  1. Sit upright, or lie down with your head supported. Let your shoulders drop.
  2. Breathe through your nose for every step.
  3. Inhale for 4, without straining to fill up.
  4. Hold for 4 with your throat soft. Don’t clamp your throat or bear down.
  5. Exhale for 4, smooth and unhurried, then rest for 4 before the next breath.
  6. If the holds feel tight, shorten them or turn them off in Adjust rhythm.

During practice

Hold4Pause at the top. Keep your throat soft.“Hold”
Exhale4Out through the nose, just as slowly.“Exhale”
Rest4Pause, empty and easy.“Rest”

Traditionally

Sama vritti means “even movement.” The Yoga Sutras describe regulating the breath by time and count, and B.K.S. Iyengar taught equal counts gradually: first an even inhale and exhale, then a pause after the inhale, and only later a pause after the exhale. The same four-count pattern spread through military and police training as “box breathing.”

Take care

  • Holds should feel easy. If one feels tight, shorten it or set it to Off.
  • If holding your breath makes you uneasy, turn the holds off and breathe evenly instead.
  • With high blood pressure, a heart or lung condition, or in pregnancy, set the holds to Off or check with your clinician first.
  • Stop and breathe normally if you feel dizzy or short of breath.
  • Never practice breath holds in or near water.

What research says

In small, short studies, mostly with healthy students, box breathing eased anxiety within a session, and one trial found it softened the body’s stress response compared with normal breathing. In the largest head-to-head trial, it did no better than mindfulness meditation and didn’t change heart rate variability. There are no long-term studies. It is not a treatment.

  • Mark Divine (2016). The breathing technique a Navy SEAL uses to stay calm and focused. TIME.
  • Balban MY et al. (2023). Brief structured respiration practices enhance mood and reduce physiological arousal. Cell Reports Medicine 4(1):100895.
  • McAllister MJ et al. (2026). Box breathing and prolonged exhalation reduces markers of physiological stress reactivity in response to a virtual Trier Social Stress Test. Comprehensive Psychoneuroendocrinology 27:100360.

Visama Vritti

Extended exhale

▶ Hear it VISH-uh-muh VRIT-tee विषम वृत्ति

GentleSeated or lyingNo breath holds

4 · Off · 6 · Off 5 min · 30 rounds · 5:00 · guided 6 breaths/min

Take care: Keep breaths soft. If the next inhale feels rushed, shorten the exhale.

A breath where the exhale is longer than the inhale. Viram starts with the gentlest form: in for 4, out for 6, with no holds.

How to

  1. Sit upright or lie down, and let your jaw and shoulders soften.
  2. Breathe in through your nose for 4.
  3. Breathe out through your nose for 6, slow and smooth, without pushing the air out.
  4. Keep the breaths soft rather than big.
  5. If you find yourself gasping for the next breath, shorten the exhale in Adjust rhythm.

Exhale6Out through the nose, slow and unhurried.“Exhale”
RestOffPause, empty and easy.“Rest”

Traditionally

Visama vritti means “uneven movement”: steps of different lengths. B.K.S. Iyengar taught it with long holds in ratios such as 1:4:2, and only under a teacher’s eye; the Gheranda Samhita counts a similar ratio. Viram keeps just the lengthened exhale, a gentle, hold-free form closer to modern teaching.

Take care

  • Keep the breaths soft. Big breaths can make you lightheaded.
  • If the next inhale feels rushed, shorten the exhale.
  • Stop and breathe normally if you feel dizzy or short of breath.
  • The classical form adds long holds. Learn it from a teacher, not from an app.

What research says

Breathing slowly, around six breaths a minute as here, reliably raises heart rate variability in the short term. Two small studies of this 4 · 6 pattern reported calmer readings and less anxiety, but neither had a comparison group. Whether the longer exhale adds anything beyond slowing down is mixed: some studies say yes, and the most careful recent one found no difference. It is not a treatment.

Nadi Shodhana

Alternate nostril breathing

▶ Hear it NAH-dee SHOH-duh-nuh नाडी शोधन

GentleSeatedNo breath holds

4 · 6 · 4 · 6 5 min · 15 rounds · 5:00 · guided 6 breaths/min

Take care: Skip it with a blocked nose. Stop if you feel dizzy.

Breathe through one nostril at a time, switching sides with your right hand. Slow, silent, and without holds.

How to

  1. Sit tall and rest your left hand on your knee.
  2. Fold the index and middle fingers of your right hand into your palm, or rest them lightly between your eyebrows.
  3. Your thumb closes the right nostril; your ring finger closes the left. A light touch is enough.
  4. Inhale left. Close left, open right. Exhale right.
  5. Inhale right. Close right, open left. Exhale left. That’s one round.
  6. Keep the breath slow and silent. When you finish, lower your hand and breathe through both nostrils.

Inhale right4Ring finger stays on the left.“Inhale right”
Exhale left6Thumb closes the right. Ring finger lifts.“Exhale left”

Traditionally

Nadi shodhana means “clearing the channels.” The Hatha Yoga Pradipika describes breathing in through one nostril and out through the other, with long holds, to purify the nadis, the body’s energy channels in yoga tradition. It is traditionally practiced before meditation, and many practitioners in India know it as Anulom Vilom. Teachers usually begin without holds, as Viram does.

Take care

  • Breathe softly; never force air through a nostril.
  • Skip it with a blocked nose, a cold, or sinus pain.
  • If the exhale feels long, make it equal to the inhale in Adjust rhythm.
  • Stop and breathe normally if you feel dizzy, anxious, or short of breath.

What research says

Most studies are small, single sessions, and many come from one research group. A review of six trials found blood pressure fell modestly, but results varied widely. Findings for anxiety are mixed, and the one study that compared it with plain slow breathing at the same pace found similar effects. It is not a treatment.

Bhramari

Humming bee breath

▶ Hear it BRAH-muh-ree भ्रामरी

GentleSeatedNo breath holds

4 · 8 5 min · 25 rounds · 5:00 · guided 5 breaths/min

Take care: Skip it with an ear or nose infection. Never press inside your ears.

Breathe in quietly, then hum softly all the way through the exhale. The long, even hum slows the breath and gives your attention a sound to rest on.

How to

  1. Sit upright with your jaw loose. Close your lips softly and keep your teeth slightly apart.
  2. If you like, press the small flaps at the front of your ears gently with your index fingers. Or rest your hands in your lap.
  3. Breathe in through your nose for 4.
  4. Breathe out for 8 while humming a low, steady “mmm.” Feel it buzz in your face and head.
  5. Keep the hum soft. It doesn’t need to be loud.
  6. When you finish, sit for a few breaths and notice the quiet.

During practice

StepsCaptionVoice
Inhale4In through the nose, quietly.“Inhale”
Hum8Lips closed. A soft, even “mmm” as you breathe out.“Hum”

Traditionally

Bhramari is named after the bhramara, a large black bee. The Hatha Yoga Pradipika lists it among eight classical breath practices, with a bee-like sound on both the inhale and the exhale. The Gheranda Samhita teaches it with the ears closed, listening for inner sounds. Modern teachers usually hum only on the exhale, as here, often before meditation.

Take care

  • Skip it with an ear infection, or a blocked or infected nose.
  • If you close your ears, press the flaps gently. Never put your fingers inside your ears.
  • Practice sitting up rather than lying down.
  • Stop and breathe normally if you feel dizzy or uncomfortable.

What research says

Most studies are small, short, and from India. They generally report lower heart rate or blood pressure after practice, but a controlled trial in people with high blood pressure found no difference from ordinary slow breathing. Humming briefly raises nitric oxide in the nose; no study links that to health. It seems about as calming as other slow breathing. It is not a treatment.

Ujjayi

Ocean breath

▶ Hear it ooj-JAH-yee उज्जायी

GentleSeated or lyingNo breath holds

5 · 5 5 min · 30 rounds · 5:00 · guided 6 breaths/min

Take care: Keep the sound soft, heard only by you. Never squeeze your throat.

Slow breathing through the nose with a gentle narrowing at the back of the throat, so each breath makes a soft, even sound, like distant waves.

How to

  1. Sit upright or lie down. Close your mouth and breathe through your nose.
  2. Gently narrow the back of your throat so the breath makes a soft, even sound, like a quiet snore.
  3. Breathe in for 5 and out for 5, keeping the sound steady both ways.
  4. Keep your face, jaw, and tongue soft. Only you need to hear it.
  5. If your throat tires, let the sound go and keep breathing slowly.

During practice

StepsCaptionVoice
Inhale5In through the nose, with a soft sound in the throat.“Inhale”
Exhale5Out through the nose, the same soft sound.“Exhale”

Traditionally

Ujjayi is often translated as “victorious.” The Hatha Yoga Pradipika describes drawing the breath in through both nostrils with a sound felt from the throat to the chest, holding it, and breathing out through the left nostril. Modern teachers such as B.K.S. Iyengar and Swami Satyananda teach it without holds, often as a first pranayama.

Take care

  • Keep your throat relaxed. The sound comes from a slight narrowing, never from squeezing.
  • If your throat feels dry or strained, drop the sound and breathe slowly.
  • Stop and breathe normally if you feel dizzy or short of breath.

What research says

Research on Ujjayi itself is thin. In one small study of beginners, slow breathing alone lowered blood pressure more clearly than slow breathing with the Ujjayi sound, which raised heart rate slightly. Much of what people feel may come from breathing slowly; the sound can help keep attention on the breath. It is not a treatment.

Based on

  • B.K.S. Iyengar (1981). Light on Pranayama. George Allen & Unwin.
  • Swami Satyananda Saraswati (2008). Asana Pranayama Mudra Bandha. Yoga Publications Trust, 4th revised edition.
  • Mason H et al. (2013). Cardiovascular and respiratory effect of yogic slow breathing in the yoga beginner: what is the best approach?. Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine 2013:743504.
  • Laborde S et al. (2022). Effects of voluntary slow breathing on heart rate and heart rate variability: a systematic review and a meta-analysis. Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews 138:104711.

Spoken introduction 56 words · about 27 s · intro.ujjayi

Sheetali

Cooling breath

▶ Hear it SHEE-tuh-lee शीतली

GentleSeatedNo breath holds

4 · 6 3 min · 18 rounds · 3:00 · guided 6 breaths/min

Take care: Skip it in cold weather, or with asthma, a cold, or low blood pressure.

Breathe in through a curled tongue, as if through a straw, and out through your nose. The incoming air feels cool on the tongue.

How to

  1. Sit upright with your head level.
  2. Stick your tongue out a little and curl the sides up into a tube.
  3. Breathe in through the tube for 4, like sipping through a straw.
  4. Draw your tongue in, close your mouth, and breathe out through your nose for 6.
  5. Can’t curl your tongue? Many people can’t. Try Sheetkari (sheet-KAH-ree): teeth lightly together, lips parted, breathe in through your teeth with a soft hiss.

During practice

StepsCaptionVoice
Inhale · mouth4Curl your tongue and sip the air in.“In through the mouth” (round 1), then “Inhale”
Exhale6Close your mouth. Out through the nose.“Exhale”

Traditionally

Sheetali means “cooling.” The Hatha Yoga Pradipika describes drawing air in over the tongue, holding it, and breathing out through the nose, and credits it with easing heat, thirst, and hunger. Curling the tongue into a tube comes from modern teaching, and the practice is traditionally kept for hot weather. Viram’s version has no hold.

Take care

  • Skip it in cold weather, or in smoky, dusty, or polluted air. Breathing through the mouth skips the nose’s warming and filtering.
  • Skip it with asthma, a cold, a cough, or a sore throat.
  • Skip it if you have low blood pressure.
  • With sensitive teeth, use the curled tongue rather than breathing through your teeth.
  • Stop and breathe normally if you feel dizzy.

What research says

Evidence is thin. A few small trials in people with high blood pressure report lower readings after weeks of practice, but none compared it with another breathing practice. The only study that measured temperature found body temperature rose slightly rather than fell; the coolness is air passing over a wet tongue. It is not a treatment.

Coherent breathing

Slow, even breathing

GentleSeated or lyingNo breath holds

5.5 · 5.5 5 min · 28 rounds · 5:08 · guided 5.5 breaths/min · half-second steps

Take care: Keep breaths gentle. If you feel lightheaded or tingly, breathe smaller.

Slow, even breaths, about five and a half a minute, in and out for the same time. There are no counts to keep: just follow the guide.

How to

  1. Sit or lie comfortably. Rest a hand on your belly if it helps.
  2. Breathe in through your nose as the guide grows, gently and without effort.
  3. Breathe out through your nose as it shrinks, just as slowly.
  4. Keep the breaths soft and roomy, not big. The pace matters more than the size.
  5. If 5.5 seconds feels long, shorten both steps in Adjust rhythm.

During practice

StepsCaptionVoice
Inhale5.5Follow the guide in, soft and slow.“Inhale”
Exhale5.5Follow the guide out. No need to empty fully.“Exhale”

Where it comes from

Researchers found that heart rate rises and falls most with the breath at around five to six breaths a minute, and call this resonance breathing. Stephen Elliott introduced Coherent Breathing, a slow, even pace like this, in 2005, and Richard Brown and Patricia Gerbarg taught it widely. The 5.5-second rhythm is a common average; each person’s ideal pace varies a little.

Take care

  • Keep breaths gentle. Lightheadedness, or tingling in your hands or lips, means you’re breathing too much: take smaller breaths or return to normal breathing.
  • It’s fine to shorten the steps. Straining isn’t the goal.
  • Stop and breathe normally if you feel dizzy or short of breath.

What research says

Slow breathing at this pace reliably raises heart rate variability during and shortly after practice. But the largest controlled trial, 400 adults over four weeks at the University of Sussex, found it improved stress, anxiety, and mood no more than a faster placebo breathing practice: both groups improved equally. Its pace hasn’t been shown to beat simpler slow breathing. It is not a treatment.

  • Fincham GW, Strauss C, Cavanagh K (2023). Effect of coherent breathing on mental health and wellbeing: a randomised placebo-controlled trial. Scientific Reports 13:22141.

Modern pattern · 4-7-8 · content v1

4-7-8 breathing

Long hold, long exhale

GentleSeated or lyingShort holds

4 · 7 · 8 · Off 4 rounds · 4 rounds · 1:16 · guided 3.2 breaths/min

Take care: Start with 4 rounds. If the hold feels hard, shorten all three counts.

Breathe in for 4, hold for 7, and breathe out through your mouth for 8. The counts are a ratio, not a test: keep them comfortable.

How to

  1. Sit with your back straight, or lie down if you’re settling for sleep.
  2. Rest the tip of your tongue on the ridge just behind your upper front teeth, and keep it there.
  3. Breathe in quietly through your nose for 4.
  4. Hold your breath gently for 7.
  5. Breathe out through your mouth for 8 with a soft whoosh. Purse your lips if that’s easier.
  6. Four rounds is enough to start. If the hold is hard, shorten all three counts together in Adjust rhythm.

During practice

StepsCaptionVoice
Inhale4In quietly through the nose.“Inhale”
Hold7Hold gently. Tongue stays behind your teeth.“Hold”
Exhale · mouth8Out through the mouth with a soft whoosh.“Out through the mouth” (round 1), then “Exhale”
RestOffPause before the next breath.“Rest”

Where it comes from

Andrew Weil, a physician, popularized this pattern and describes it as adapted from pranayama. He teaches the counts as a ratio rather than a speed, suggests four breaths at a time, twice a day, and building to eight only after a month.

Take care

  • Mild lightheadedness is common at first. If it happens, stop and breathe normally.
  • The hold should never strain. Shorten all three counts rather than forcing it.
  • With a heart or lung condition, or in pregnancy, check with your clinician first, or choose a practice without holds.
  • Never practice breath holds in or near water.

What research says

Evidence is thin. Small trials, mostly around surgery or medical procedures, report less anxiety compared with routine care, but not compared with a placebo practice. In the one direct comparison, slow breathing at six breaths a minute raised heart rate variability more than 4-7-8. No rigorous trial has tested it for falling asleep. It is not a treatment.
